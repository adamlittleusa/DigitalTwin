Adam's own account of his career, from his recorded monologue, in the order he tells it.

I spent close to twenty years in the Army doing intelligence operations, from enlisting in 2001 to retiring in 2018, between active duty and the Guard. I specialized in human intelligence collection, which is essentially anything that has to do with talking to people and collecting intelligence, and I did a lot of team building: putting together small special-purpose teams for operations and mixing and matching specialties across them.

Around 2016 I started thinking about what I wanted to do when I retired. I knew I wanted cybersecurity, but I wasn't sure how to make that analog-to-digital jump. At the time I was on the insider threat team for Afghanistan at Fort Devens, Massachusetts. We were using an early low-code automation tool, a SOAR before SOARs existed, and it broke. Our mission involved crunching big data. Out of a mixture of desperation and curiosity I went into our electronic library, found a book on VBA, and taught myself enough on the fly to build Excel macros that batched the work through in smaller data sets and got the same outcome as the original tool. That was my aha moment: I realized I could do the digital stuff. So I jumped into cybersecurity face first.

From there I did a role at MIT Lincoln Laboratory, mostly auditing and ISSO work, where I learned the compliance side of the house: RMF, 800-53, NIST, the whole works. It was special being at MIT, but I worked in the basement of a basement and never saw sunlight, and I knew I couldn't keep that up long term.

So I sat down and Googled "machine learning plus cybersecurity", and Recorded Future came up. Three days later I was employee 51. I was one of their early customer success people, got a lot of traction, and grew into the principal customer success role and then strategic accounts. While I was managing strategic accounts I got the chance to build something on the side. Clients kept telling me they couldn't have the ROI conversation with their budget holders and CISOs. Recorded Future exposed usage metrics through its APIs, but the teams using it weren't engineers and couldn't pull that data themselves. I had just enough Python and JavaScript to do something about it, so I built a small project that pulled the metrics and produced reports. Before long I was supporting well over a hundred clients with weekly and monthly reports. It became the largest tagged Jira in the company's history, and the product team ended up building a dedicated page in the UI for the concept.

That led to the product side. Recorded Future created a product architect role for me, sitting between the field and the data science team and working directly with the head data scientist. I translated value from the field into what actually got built and shipped, and I worked with the head data scientist to evaluate novel data sets for their intelligence value and what it would take to productize them. I loved that work.

Through it I saw a bigger CTI problem further upstream: advisory. Teams were spending serious money on threat intelligence data, and we had solved getting the data into their hands, but most of those teams grew out of IT operations rather than the intelligence community. They didn't have the background to build and manage intelligence operations beyond the technical side, so they couldn't fully operationalize what they were buying. I coined that CTI advisory and made the case that it was the next big problem. The company agreed it was real but it wasn't a business they wanted to build; that is what firms like Accenture do. So I decided to be brave, jump out, and go solve it myself.

I took a couple of CTI leadership roles first because I wanted to experience what it takes to build these programs operationally, for real, from scratch. I didn't plan it this way, but I ended up at Accenture with the opportunity to build that advisory service directly. I built it from the ground up: the decks and workshop materials, the talk tracks, the delivery design, the pricing, the initial proposals and statements of work. I sold it within the Accenture ecosystem to a number of marquee brands, Verizon among them, then built a team and managed the delivery.

Toward the end of my time at Accenture I got the AI bug. I was doing extra work on the side with the Secure AI team, AI threat landscaping, and decided I wanted to get smart, work fully AI native, and build AI things and AI security things, because I think that's a big, big problem. That's how the Corelight role came about: they recruited me to help build their AI story and strategy. And that brings us to today.
